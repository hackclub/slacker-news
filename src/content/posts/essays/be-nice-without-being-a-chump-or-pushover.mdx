title: How to Be Nice Without Being a Chump or a Pushover
---

As a kid, I wasn’t nice. I wasn’t mean either. But the people telling me to be nice were adults, and most adults were chumps.

They meant well. But they were clueless. They were excited for stuff I hated. They wanted me to read Shakespeare, solve math problems, and play sports. In the sports, people threw stuff at my face. This is fun, the adults explained patiently. They were also useless in a conflict: other kids would do something bad to me, I'd ask adults for help, and the adults--unable to figure out who was telling the truth--would give me a "both sides" homily. A few adults were better than this, and I am grateful for them. But overall I learned by observation that being nice meant being a chump.

When I became an adult, I met another nice archetype: the pushover. Pushovers have all kinds of stories for why they let other people treat them like garbage: I don’t want to cause trouble, it’s really no big deal, I need these people because no one else wants me. Many stories, many roads, one destination: they get used remorselessly, again and again, by the worst people they meet.

Because they are obvious, chumps and pushovers were the face of kindness, at least for me. Small wonder I didn’t want to be like them.

Thing is, there are other, better ways to be kind. But they are easy to miss, and therefore hard to learn. But slowly, I started to notice people being kind without making a fool of themselves.

I see a lot of kindness at Hack Club. I also see a lack of kindness, which I don't chalk up to meanness. When I see Hack Clubbers lacking kindness, I assume you are making the same calculation I did at your age: being nice requires being an idiot, so I'm not gonna be *mean*, but I'm not gonna be nice either.

There is a better way, and I want to share it with you. So I went around and I asked some of the nicest people at Hack Club: “How do you be kind to people?”

**Felix**

I wanted to interview Felix because he is a master of de-escalation. Many a time, people have been pissed at each other in a thread, and Felix steps in to say something that everyone finds satisfying, or at least gets people to treat each other well. He made three points:

First, In conflict, use ["I statements"](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/facdev-medicine/files/2011/08/I-messages-handout.pdf). E.g. instead of "it is annoying that we've...", say "I feel kind of annoyed that we've...". The idea is that whereas one places blame (“you're being annoying”), the other focuses on my experiences.

Second, separate understanding their feelings, understanding their argument, and agreeing with their opinion. I acknowledge that I understand their argument or how they feel, by saying things like "yea that's fair". It doesn't mean I agree, just that I get their viewpoint and can see why they think that.

Third, there's a distinction to make between nice and kind.<sup><a href="#note-1" id="ref-1">1</a></sup> Being nice is performative and often not a reflection of who someone is. It's about "keeping the peace", like when people say "Just be nice okay?" Being kind is genuine and also leaves room to be upset. I'm not being nice when I say "I feel kind of annoyed..." but I am being kind.

**Rebeka**

Most Hack Clubbers don’t know Rebeka, because she works more with HQ staff. I will always be grateful to her for how much she helped me when I first moved to Vermont; she even found me a place to live.

Rebeka is also quietly badass. Before Hack Club, she worked with homeless people. Some of them had serious problems that also made it hard for others to be around them. Rebeka had to learn how to stand up for herself respectfully, even when people were screaming at her and cursing her out.<sup><a href="#note-2" id="ref-2">2</a></sup> She said:

I like to ask people a question or two to learn more about who they are, based on what they’ve already hinted they are interested in. When I pick these questions, I watch myself to make sure I’m not being competitive. I like hiking, but I wouldn’t ask “Have you climbed a 4000 footer?”

I also try to emulate all the people who made me feel comfortable and good. When I was younger, I even copied the language and body language of people whom I admired. If it’s an in-person interaction, I try to smile!

**Meghana**

Give other people the benefit of the doubt, that they don’t know they are being rude or annoying. Assume that just like me, they want to build cool things.

**Lou**

If you appreciate something about someone, even if it’s tiny, tell them.

If you are having a text conversation and all your responses are all very short, every once in a while send a full sentence with a thank you or a smile or something.

Put a space before an exclamation mark !

**Wally**

Most conversations I have are with Hack Clubbers I've seen before but don't know that well. In those cases, I try to constantly evaluate the conversation as it's going to hopefully have a "successful" one. If it's turning into an argument or becoming uncomfortable, I'd rather end it while it's still successful-ish than let it turn into an FD case.

**Zach**

Being nice makes everything better. I’ve spent a lot of time in spaces where I was younger than other people, and there were huge power differences. Power, both explicit and implicit, asserts itself everywhere. I want a social contract where everyone gets to decide who they want to be. That’s not the default though. It only happens if you make it happen. I remember how specific people treated me when I was living alone in SF at the age of 15. Some were very nice. Some were exploitative. I remember all of them. Hack Clubbers will go on to have outsize influence in the world. When you are an adult, are you going to be like the person who, when they saw me sleeping on a couch at 16 years old, spontaneously invited me to join a Thiel fellows orientation week even though I wasn’t one yet? Or are you going to be like the executive who tried to get me drunk as a 15-year-old?

Being nice is really hard. But I think it’s not about telling people what they want to hear, but treating them how you’d want to be treated.

**Me**

Once I started noticing people who are good at being kind, I consciously adopted their ways. And I started learning from my experiences: how others made me feel, times when I helped people, and times when I hurt them. And as a debate coach, I started noticing common patterns that led to pointless, stupid debates. I slowly built a set of habits to be kind and have productive disagreements. Some of them have already been said. Here is what remains:

- Asking a lot of questions about people’s opinions, especially if I think I disagree with someone.

- Focusing on what people are doing, without making assumptions about why they are doing it.

- Explaining my reasoning and giving people the chance to challenge me.

- Keeping criticism and concerns in DMs to avoid public embarrassment.

- When someone doesn’t know something obvious, remembering that I don’t know stuff that is obvious to other people.

- Giving people a chance to express their feelings without interruption. Much frustration comes from not being or not feeling heard.

- Giving people openings to say things that they might be thinking, but are afraid to say.

- Asking people “What can I do to help?” or “Is there anything I can do to make things easier for you?”

The result is not perfect. But I don’t feel like a pushover or a chump, and I feel proud of the hard work I put into learning these habits. And though I wasn’t expecting it when I undertook to learn them, kindness has actually had a huge personal payoff. Kind people can sense each other, and gravitate together. All of my friends are really kind, and as a result I’ve had life-affirming experiences of love and trust, safety and acceptance, and near zero drama. It really is a better way to live.
