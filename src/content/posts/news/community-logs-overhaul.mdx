From today, the Fire Department will be discontinuing individual public logs in <SlackChannel id="community-logs" />. In their place, we’ll be publishing monthly Transparency Reports to give you a clear picture of how the Fire Department operates. We realise this is quite a contentious change; however, after months of deliberation, we believe this change is the best way to balance privacy and safety, with transparency.

## Why We’re Making This Change

The <SlackChannel id="community-logs" /> channel was one of the Fire Department’s attempts at being more open with the decisions we take. The intention behind publicly logging major infractions was to minimise confusion about why you are not able to communicate with a Hack Clubber anymore, and to encourage productive discussion whilst minimising speculation in other channels.

The reality is, <SlackChannel id="community-logs" /> has steered away from that direction. Each log became fuel for gossip about individuals whom we took action on, which often led to toxicity hurting the person in question or the community in general. We’ve dealt with cases with Hack Clubbers going out of their way to dox/find personal information about people who’ve appeared in <SlackChannel id="community-logs" />, and in some cases, Hack Clubbers making alt accounts posing as someone who we’ve banned to cause trouble. These public logs have created flamewars where people who never would’ve involved themselves or who had only a passing interest felt obligated to become a part of the conversation.

From the perspective of someone appearing in <SlackChannel id="community-logs" />, it can feel like a permanent stain on your reputation. When a LYLA record is publicly logged, it remains there forever. We believe that people can change over time and learn from their mistakes, but they shouldn’t have that looming over their head for the rest of their time in Hack Club.

## What Will Transparency Reports Look Like?

These Transparency Reports will take on the form of formal publications, released on a monthly basis through a medium that is yet to be determined as of now.

They’ll contain information like:

- How the Fire Department works, and how we take actions
- How many reports are received over time
- How many LYLA records are created over time
- How long it takes to resolve cases
- What actions are taken
- How many LYLA records are created across categories

### What won’t they have?

Transparency Reports won’t contain any information on specific cases. They won’t contain usernames, evidence, decision-making, or anything along these lines. The aim is to preserve the privacy of those involved whilst being as transparent as we feasibly can.

### Isn’t That Less Transparent?

We’d actually argue that, in some ways, this approach is more transparent – and we’d encourage you to think about it for a second: what do you actually gain from knowing Slack User 24367 was banned for fraud?

Transparency Reports allow us to give you an insight into how the Fire Department takes actions without jeopardising the privacy of other Hack Clubbers. You get to see how we’re resolving reports, how long they’re taking us to resolve, and how these translate into actions taken. This is all information that you wouldn’t have normally been able to see with the old <SlackChannel id="community-logs" />.

## On Accountability

Accountability is a term thrown around a lot here on Slack. The general idea is that “with community-logs, we can ensure that the Fire Department is making fair decisions.” While we’re all in favour of being held accountable for our mistakes, there’s really not much you can do without the full context.

We’ve seen it before: <SlackChannel id="meta" /> posts from or on behalf of people who’ve been banned for one reason or another, but with only one side of the story shown and many crucial details left out. This isn’t accountability at all. If you don’t have the full context of a case, a simple log with a brief reason doesn’t give you enough information to tell if the Fire Department has made the right decision.

Transparency Reports give you a picture of how we operate, the volume of our work, and the patterns in our decision-making, without putting Hack Clubbers on display.

## How Do I Know What Happened to XYZ?

There’s a pretty big difference between someone making the decision to tell people about what’s happened to them and us forcefully broadcasting that for all to see. If a Hack Clubber truly wants people to know that they were banned or shushed, they’ll make their own choice to get the word out.

At the end of the day, the Fire Department’s goal isn’t retribution; it’s rehabilitation. Hack Clubbers who want to show change will recognise their mistakes and make change, and this is a private, developmental matter between them and us that the community doesn’t need to know about. If they want to make that public, it’s their choice, but it’s incredibly important that we make it their choice.

## How We Got Here

A couple of years ago, the Fire Department created the <SlackChannel id="community-logs" /> channel for the purpose of logging major conduct infractions, including shushes and temporary or permanent bans. The Slack was a vastly different community then, where conduct actions were usually unheard of and bans were big news – @radioblahaj [“Bans are always the last resort."](https://hackclub.slack.com/archives/C0188CY57PZ/p1733846220460049?thread_ts=1733843796.477329&cid=C0188CY57PZ)

Since then, Hack Club has grown significantly. The Slack now has an estimated 200,000 members, the Fire Department deals with hundreds of conduct reports a week, and <SlackChannel id="community-logs" /> has become something very different from what it was originally intended to be.

We made <SlackChannel id="community-logs" /> read-only a while back, which helped reduce some of the heated conversations; however, that did not solve the underlying problem.

## A Note on “Transparency”

“Transparency” is a word that gets thrown around a lot. There is a high expectation for the Fire Department to be open and held accountable. We try to act on that feedback, and this change is part of that.

However, it’s important to establish what transparency means in this context, and what Hack Clubbers often expect from the Fire Department is “full transparency”. That would involve giving the community access to each Shroud report (and who submitted it), all the details and evidence in that case and any other reports or logs involving that person, so that Hack Clubbers can decide on their own accord whether the Fire Department’s action was appropriate. Obviously, we can’t do that, so what is the next level of transparency we can offer?

What we _can_ do is be open about how we operate and the decisions we make, without exposing private details, to provide that assurance and accountability, and still give the community the opportunity to give feedback on the actions we take – that is what Transparency Reports are designed to do.

Transparency does not mean you are entitled to knowing every single piece of information or being able to stick your nose where it does not belong. Every piece of private information revealed in the name of transparency necessarily compromises some individual’s privacy, and unfortunately there is no perfect solution here, but we’re always open to feedback on how we can do better.

## Final Thoughts

This is a big change, and we know it won’t land without questions, nor will it be perfect, and that’s okay. We’ve tried to be as open as we can about our reasoning here, and Transparency Reports are our commitment to keeping that openness every month.
