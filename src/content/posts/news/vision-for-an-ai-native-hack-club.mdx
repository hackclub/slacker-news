AI is changing what it means to be a programmer. Soon it will change what it means to be a hardware engineer too.

A year ago, the projects in Hack Club that most relied on AI were slop. Today, many of the coolest projects built by Hack Clubbers are 100% written by AI - like [cs2go.3kh0.net](https://cs2go.3kh0.net/), a Counter-Strike 2 port to the browser by Rowan, age 17.

These cool projects take actual skill to build. You can’t one-shot them. Most people who use AI aren’t yet capable of building them. And learning how to use AI effectively to build stuff like this is actually a lot like learning to code. It’s a distinct skill, you get better at it through practice, and it applies across building many different types of projects.

Many of you probably heard about some of the Millennium Prize Problems being solved with AI. I’ve been enjoying reading Terrance Tao’s [blog](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/). He’s a famous mathematician and talks about how he uses AI. Interestingly, the way he uses AI is very similar to the way I see the most effective builders I know using it - and it’s different from how most people use AI. He and other mathematicians are practicing the same meta-skill that the best AI builders like Rowan in Hack Club have developed.

It’s clear that if Hack Club wants to help people become technically skilled, form lifelong friendships, and build personal agency, we need to have a better approach to AI than how we handle it today. If some of the coolest projects made by Hack Clubbers don’t count in most Hack Club programs, we need to improve our approach.

But how do we do that without rewarding slop?

Here’s my pitch for an AI native Hack Club:

1. We must solve AI time tracking. Hours of productive struggle toward shipped projects is still the right metric because one-shotting takes 2 minutes of productive struggle while intense reverse engineering takes dozens of hours, but [Hackatime](https://hackatime.hackclub.com/) doesn’t currently count how much time you are actively solving problems when building with AI. It just counts how active your AI agent is. We must either improve Hackatime to count this time accurately, or develop a new tool that can answer the question: “how many hours of productive struggle did I spend building this project?“. Importantly, not all hours of screen time or time prompting is productive struggle - if you are in [dark flow](https://www.fast.ai/posts/2026-01-28-dark-flow/) or doing the same thing over and over again, that shouldn’t count.

2. We must do way more in-person events. How can we create a world where events like [Hack Club Haven](https://haven.hackclub.com/) happen 5 times per year? Last weekend, 6,017 teenagers checked into an in-person Haven event on [Hack Club Attend](https://attend.hackclub.com/), 2,161 games were made by 4,843 teens for the theme “Bounce Back”, and 2,864 people gave feedback with an average [NPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_promoter_score) of 71 (scored from -100 to 100). That is absolutely incredible and probably the largest and highest quality high school game jam of all time! And there are 13 more Havens happening next weekend! We need to be doing stuff like this all the time. Hats off to the Haven team.

3. We must change the definition for what counts as a shipped project in Hack Club to include: “The project must do what it says it does”. The biggest problem with most AI slop projects is that they don’t actually work. Previously, whether a technical project was deployed was a pretty good proxy for whether it did what it said it did. Most people who put in the effort to ship their project also tested it to make sure it actually worked. AI has made it way easier to ship projects, so this is no longer a good enough proxy and we must start checking this ourselves.

4. We must introduce external feedback to You Ship, We Ships. My belief is that if you want to become great at building projects, the trifecta is as follows: 1) you must ship lots of projects, 2) you must spend many hours of productive struggle on those projects, 3) you must get and incorporate [feedback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feedback) from the outside world into your projects. Today we measure #1 and #2, but don’t yet measure #3 even though all the best projects in Hack Club include it.

   We need to change this. The more hours of productive struggle you spend, the more you need feedback feeding back into your projects. That can look like getting actual users, posting your work on Reddit and getting people to engage with it, or just having instrumentation in place to see that your project actually works (ex. if you say your app has a login function, there needs to be analytics showing that people have actually logged in and used it - even if it’s a small number of people).

   What if we built a free service like Hackatime that was kind of like [Posthog](https://posthog.com/)? Hack Clubbers could incorporate it into their apps and through that, they - and we - could see their projects getting external feedback through usage.

5. We must continue running incredible, life-changing, high-agency adventures like [Hack Club Fallout](https://youtu.be/dTGFOKblRc4) in China, [Hack Club Undercity](https://youtu.be/kaEFv7e49mo) in San Francisco, [The Hacker Zephyr](https://youtu.be/2BID8_pGuqA) in a train across the USA, and [Hack Club Infill](https://youtu.be/5G_g6yMLbMs) in Denver. So many Hack Clubbers have formed their closest friendships and had formative experiences like traveling alone for the first time through these events.

Join upcoming ones like [Hack Club Phantom](https://phantom.hackclub.com/) in Philadelphia, [Hack Club Taiyaki](https://taiyaki.hackclub.com/) in Japan, [Hack Club Fallen](https://fallen.hackclub.com/) in China, and [Hack Club Crossroads](https://crossroads.hackclub.com/) in a train across Europe!
