---
title: Journals aren't Timesheets
author: "@tanuki"
date: 2026-07-31
---
How do you measure effort? Many hardware programs answer that question with journals. Today, I want to question the assumption that journals are the right way to measure effort on hardware projects.

To be clear, I do not think journals should disappear. In fact, I think they are one of the best parts of Hack Club hardware.

I enjoy writing and reading journals. Journals document progress, encourage reflection, teach other people, and create a public record of what someone built. They allow people outside of Hack Club to follow along with a project and understand the work that went into it.

However, while journals are excellent for documenting work, I do not believe they are a reliable way to measure effort. My argument is not that journaling should go away, but that journals should not be used as a quantitative system for counting hours.

**Scalability Is The Real Problem**

To have any meaningful understanding of how long something should take, you need experience. The response is usually that we need better reviewers, and I agree. However, this does not solve the underlying problem. It assumes that a large supply of highly experienced reviewers exists and can simply be recruited.

In reality, experienced hardware reviewers can be scarce. Most highly experienced builders are busy building. Even Blueprint, which had some of the strongest reviewers in Hack Club, struggled with review throughput. If accurate reviewing requires a relatively small pool of highly experienced people, I do not think that scales as hardware and Hack Club continues to grow, with 100k new people with Stardance, and 2.5 times more gap years this year.

This becomes even more concerning as hardware grows. Maintaining consistency across dozens of programs would require significant auditing and coordination. The system becomes increasingly dependent on everyone making similar judgment calls, despite those judgments being inherently subjective.

**Manipulation and Consistency**

Construct's initial journal inflation, where some organizers specifically inflated time under the pretense that “people often don’t know how long something took” is a proven case study showing that journals are really easy to manipulate. This remained under-the-radar for months, and was only stopped due to their other actions. Even if we can trust certain people to harshly deflate and keep things roughly accurate, that consistency never remains. Organizers can be very light on deflation, and in most cases I do not see how that would ever be caught.

**Accuracy and Verification**

Solely journal-based systems are fundamentally subjective. The use of solely journaling for deflation is prone to manipulation and bias, and can simply be inaccurate. They should not be used for a quantitative measurement of effort.

Journals are realistically guesses as far as deflation goes. A reviewer can never truly know how long work took. Even if reviewers have experience and a good understanding of hardware, they are still estimating.

When I review projects, it always feels messy. First I look at their other projects to understand their technical depth. Then I compare it to my own experience, my understanding of the average hardware person, how detailed their logs are, and my general knowledge of the topic. Then I estimate how long it took and compare that to their journaled time and justification. Even if this process makes reviews more accurate, it doesn’t prove accuracy.

One thing that consistently concerns me is that many arguments for journal accuracy ultimately rely on intuition. A common response is that experienced reviewers know how long things normally take. That doesn’t prove it’s accurate; it just shows that having experienced reviewers is better than not having experienced reviewers. Similarly, people often point to Blueprint and say journaling works there. My question is always the same: what data can be used to support that it works while avoiding circular reasoning?

I’ve heard arguments like "the expected time for a Hackpad is 8 to 10 hours and that’s what gets reported." But isn’t the expected time itself based on self-reported time? Additionally, Hackpads are recurring projects that are much easier to analyze than almost every other project.

The issue is that journals are difficult to verify. If someone adds 15 minutes to a journal, then 15 minutes to the next one, then 15 minutes to the next one, how does that become obvious? Even something like five extra minutes per journal can add up significantly over the course of a project. Additionally, "stupid" things can take large amounts of time; the same task can vary dramatically depending on the person’s experience level. Among beginners, something that takes one beginner 30 minutes might take another beginner 3 hours; hard things don’t challenge different people the same way.

@Aarav\ J, a Forge organizer, captured the core problem clearly: "Right now the review process is so much so 100% guessing as neither party can accurately verify how many hours they really spent on the project. [...] Adding 10 or 15 minutes here and there to a journal will compound and eventually give massive gains." His experience with timelapsing as an alternative is instructive: "In my opinion it makes life SO much easier for reviewers as we don’t have to guess – we can start validating their time based on real metrics that we receive from their lapse."

**Where I Think Hardware Should Go**

To be clear, I do not think journals should disappear. In fact, I think they are one of the best parts of Hack Club hardware.

However, I think journals should become documentation rather than be used for counting hours. They are excellent at documenting progress, teaching others, and showing work publicly. What they are not excellent at is proving how many hours somebody spent on a project. For example, Fallout uses a hybrid of journal-based documentation and timelapsing. Breadboard uses a mix of journal-based documentation and on-platform time tracking.

These systems have worked tremendously well; hours have become very easily recordable, verifiable, and real, with users having tracked 69,754 hours through Fallout.

When hardware was smaller and more experimental, journaling was a sufficient solution for coordinating effort at that scale.

That context has changed: hardware is now much larger and operating under conditions where informal judgment and self-reported quantitative tracking no longer remain consistent or scalable.

As a result, Hack Club hardware should move away from relying on journal-based quantitative measurement and continue actively replacing it with systems that are more verifiable and robust. Journals should remain, but their role as a mechanism for measuring effort is no longer justified by the demands of the current system.
